

Here is a standard English prose rendering:

Touma noticed the wrongness first. The old transit station was too bright, the lights cutting sharp angles, and the vending machines hummed too loudly. Strangers moved through the space without looking, without becoming involved. He was tired, and he had been used to trouble long enough to know the rule: trouble outside the body could be outrun, but trouble inside the skull could not. He was not heroic.

The headache was already blooming. It had extra edges, precise as a paperknife laid along the edge of thought. That was the first warning. The second warning followed. A small-built teenager with a school bag stood near the service gate, his face gone blank with terror. The recruiter in the gray coat moved with procedural calm, one hand inside the coat, and then that hand closed around Touma’s wrist.

For half a second, the station became a tableau: the sharp lights, the hum, the boy’s school bag, the gray coat, the hand around the wrist, the hand inside the coat, the service gate. Then the second half arrived.

Touma recognized the boy. It was not gallantry. It was recognition. Six steps separated them. “Hey,” he said. The thought he needed to keep was simple: keep moving, keep the kid quiet, finish the handoff. But the thought was unmade, then released. He stumbled. Relief came only halfway. The other half was violation, a clean, quiet cruelty, a pin through the inside of the world, a private appointment with gravity. A memory hiccuped, and something familiar was lost. The boy’s identity and any prior connection were not revealed, but the loss was real.

Plain-English note: The scene is meant to be lean in movement but heavy with tension; the emotional arc moves from wrongness to recognition, intervention, relief, violation, and loss.
