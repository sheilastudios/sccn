Unthink — Chapter 1 Opening

The headache was already blooming behind his left eye when he came down the stairs into the old transit station. That was the first warning. He'd had enough of them to know the sequence by now, and knowing the sequence never once stopped it from arriving.

The lights were too sharp. The vending machines hummed too loud, or maybe just loud in a way that had teeth in it tonight. Every edge in the room looked like it had a second edge behind it, the way things did when the headache was coming — not doubled, exactly, but sharpened, as though someone had taken a paperknife to the edge of every thought and trimmed the backing off so you could see what held it in place.

Touma kept moving. That was the rule. Trouble outside the skull was outrunnable. Trouble inside it was not, but you could still walk through a room like a person who had somewhere to be, and most of the time the room let you.

The station was the usual late-shift population: a woman with a rolling bag reading her phone, two men on the bench who weren't waiting for a train, a cleaner pushing a cart along the far platform with the resigned rhythm of someone whose job had outlived the infrastructure that justified it. None of them were involved in anything. That was the point of strangers.

The recruiter was standing near the service gate.

Gray coat. That was the first thing — not dramatic, not cinematic, just a gray coat with the collar turned up, hands in his pockets, one of them doing something inside the fabric that wasn't fidgeting. Procedural. The posture of a man who had done this enough times that the doing of it had become a kind of paperwork.

Touma saw the boy first.

Small-built, teenaged, a school bag on one shoulder that he was holding by the strap the way kids hold things when they've been told to carry something important and haven't yet decided if they're going to. He was standing three meters from the recruiter, and his face had the particular blankness that terror produces when it has run past expression and arrived somewhere flatter and quieter. Not screaming. Not crying. The blankness of a system that had shut down its displays to conserve whatever was left.

The recruiter's hand came out of his coat.

That was the second warning. Not the hand itself — hands came out of coats — but the way it came out: already shaped around something, already aimed, the wrist turning so the grab would land on the boy's forearm with the precision of a man who knew exactly how much resistance a startled teenager could produce and had already subtracted it from the motion.

Touma moved.

Not forward. Not yet. He shifted his weight off the wall where he'd been leaning and took one step that put him in the line between the recruiter's sight and the nearest exit, which was also the line between the recruiter and the boy. It was not a dramatic step. It was the step of a tired man adjusting his position, which is the most invisible thing a tired man can do in a room full of people who are not tired.

The recruiter's hand closed around the boy's wrist.

The boy didn't make a sound. His mouth opened and nothing came out, the way a door opens onto a room where the furniture has already been removed.

Six steps. That was the distance. Six steps between Touma and the hand on the wrist, and he took them at the pace of a man walking toward a departure board he was mildly interested in, which was the pace that no one in the history of transit stations had ever found alarming.

"Hey."

One word. Not loud. Not a challenge. The verbal equivalent of stepping on a floorboard in a quiet house — enough to change what the room was paying attention to, not enough to announce that you'd meant to.

The recruiter looked up.

And Touma looked back, and what he did next was not gallantry. He wanted to be clear about that, even if only to himself, because the word would have embarrassed him and the truth wouldn't. What he did was recognition. He recognized the shape of the thing happening — the hand inside the coat, the hand on the wrist, the boy's blank face, the service gate behind them all — because he had seen it before, or because the headache had, or because the two had become difficult to separate, which was itself a kind of seeing.

He reached for the place where the headache lived.

Not with his hands. With the part of him that the headache was already touching, the part that felt like a pin pushed through the inside of the world and into the soft tissue of whatever he was when he wasn't being a man in a transit station with a bad eye and a worse history. He reached for it the way you'd reach for a tool you kept in a drawer you didn't like opening — knowing where it was, knowing what it would cost, knowing that the cost was the thing you were about to pay.

The headache sharpened. For half a second the room went very clean and very quiet, the way a room goes when someone has removed the noise from it not by silencing it but by cutting the thing that carried the noise, like severing a thread that held a whole curtain of sound. The edges came off the vending machines. The hum went flat. The cleaner's cart stopped squeaking, though the cleaner kept pushing it, because what had stopped wasn't the cart but the part of the world that translated the squeaking into something Touma's ears could receive.

It was a private appointment with gravity, the kind where you arrive alone and the other party doesn't show up because you are the other party, and the appointment is with the part of you that can reach into the space between a man's intention and his hand and remove the intention like a wire from a circuit.

The recruiter's grip loosened.

Not because he decided to let go. Because the thing that had been making his hand close was no longer being made. His fingers opened with the slow confusion of a hand that has lost the argument it was having with the object it was holding, and the boy's wrist slid free.

The boy stumbled backward. His bag swung wide. He caught the strap with his other hand, and for a moment his face came back online — not relief, not yet, but the particular expression of a person who has just discovered that the floor is still where he left it.

The first half of the relief arrived.

Then the second half arrived.

The second half was always worse. The headache didn't just take something out of the room; it took something out of him, and what it took this time was a piece of the continuity between one moment and the next, the small invisible thread that let a person know they had been the same person thirty seconds ago that they were now. He blinked, and the station reassembled itself around him — lights, hum, strangers, the cleaner's cart, the recruiter standing with his open hand still raised as though waiting for someone to explain what it had been holding — but there was a gap.

A memory hiccup. Not a lost memory. A lost familiarity. The boy was looking at him, and Touma was looking at the boy, and the boy was someone Touma should have recognized — not from the station, not from tonight, but from the shape of a thing that had been there before the headache, before the six steps, before the hand inside the coat — and that recognition had been in his hands a moment ago, in the motion of reaching for the drawer, and now it was gone, cleanly and quietly, the way a thing is gone when it has been removed not by forgetting but by something with a more precise kind of cruelty.

The recruiter's hand went back into the coat.

Touma's eye throbbed.

The headache had already been blooming. Now it settled in, the way a guest settles in who has no intention of leaving and has brought luggage. The edges came back to the vending machines. The hum returned, louder than before, or the same as before and his tolerance was simply lower.

The boy was still standing there. The blankness had cracked, and under it was something that might have been gratitude or might have been the first stage of a different kind of terror — the kind that comes when you realize the person who helped you is not entirely the kind of person who helps, or is, but the helping has a shape you don't recognize and a cost you can see in the way they're standing, which is the way people stand when they've just paid for something at a counter that doesn't give change.

Touma kept moving. That was still the rule.
