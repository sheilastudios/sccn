

Here is a standard English prose rendering of the packet:

Mio looked away too quickly.

Touma saw it before he could stop seeing it. It was not that people were failing to see her. It was that, if they saw her, they might classify her. The safehouse bedroom was cheap and narrow: a desk, a chair, the edge of a futon, the air conditioner running so quietly it seemed to be holding its breath. The doorway stood open to the hall, and the privacy of the room was already failing in stages.

Nico was still at the edge of the space, uncomfortable in a way that made him decent enough to look away. He was not built for this kind of intimacy.

“This isn’t a normal exchange,” Nico said.

“It’s like they’re removing a nervous system,” Mio said.

“Don’t phrase it like that,” Touma said.

Nico shifted. “I should go.”

He left because he was uncomfortable, and after he was gone the room became smaller.

Touma gestured to the chair by the desk. “Sit.”

Mio looked at the old projector on the desk. “Is that thing haunted?”

“If it is, it’s at least consistent.”

“Comforting.”

She sat.

The chair squeaked.

The squeak was acceptance. Touma felt relief before he had time to be embarrassed by it. The furniture had not rejected her. The room had not rejected her. The mundane world had made space for her, and it mattered disproportionately.

“Don’t make that face,” he said.

Mio’s face did something quick and embarrassed. Her fear looked like embarrassment, with a face.

“It’s not funny,” Touma said.

“It’s not supposed to be funny.”

“Then don’t do the joke.”

He understood, then, that the fear was under the jokes.

“Can you eat?” he asked.

Mio looked at him as if the question had a body.

He realized he did not know. Her body was a set of unknowns shaped like questions. He wanted to take her hand. He stopped.

She noticed he had been about to ask.

From the hall, Nico tried the outer door. The latch caught, and for a second the cheap frame blocked him. Then it gave, and he was allowed out.

“I was going to ask if it hurt,” Touma said.

The room did something halfway between a glitch and a flinch. The light near the doorway thinned. The air conditioner hiccuped. The projector image on the wall split for a moment, as if the room were trying too hard to hold a shape that made sense.

Mio’s finger twitched.

Touma noticed.

“Ground rules,” he said.

Mio looked at him.

“No surprise touching,” he said. “Tell me if something hurts.”

“I don’t want to be a problem.”

“People are problems, humanly.”

She was quiet for a moment. “If I say something is wrong, you’ll listen?”

“Yes.”

“Really?”

“Yes.”

It wasn’t trust yet. But he could tell she wanted to believe it was possible.

The support technician appeared in the doorway.

That was the wrong kind of miracle.

“Who is that?” Mio asked.

She was perfectly still.

Touma stood too fast. “Not your business,” he said to the technician.

Embarrassment and fear arrived together.

The technician’s face changed in the way Touma recognized now as gossip taking shape. A private thing was becoming a story. A social story. A vector.

“Leave,” Touma said.

The technician left.

That had been public. Visible. Humiliating. The privacy was false now.

“So that’s what I am,” Mio said. “To everyone who just saw this.”

It was a dramatic reveal line, but it was not a joke.

“Too many eyes,” Touma said. “Too late for a rumor.”

“What do we do if people start asking?”

“We lie.”

“Disgust? Affection?”

Mio’s voice stayed level. “If I am going to be seen, I refuse to be seen as a mistake.”

Touma looked at her. Being visible did not make her an anomaly or a symptom.

“You are not a mistake.”

It was dangerous, because it was sincere.

A small shaky laugh came out of her.

For one second, the room was bearable.

The warmth did not cancel the fear.

Then there was a soft scraping outside the room.

It was not footsteps. It was not the door.

It sounded like a fingertip dragging on the other side of the wall.

There was pressure in the plaster.

A colder limiter pulse ran through Touma, a warning without a source. The system was no longer just surveillance. It had a listening presence that filled the space, and the pressure of classification had become spatial.

“Don’t let the system decide what I am,” Mio said.

The wall answered with a deeper scrape.

It sounded heard, and leaning closer.
