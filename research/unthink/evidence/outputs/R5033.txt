

Mio looked away too quickly.

It was not a glance. It was a correction, as if the air between them had become a room with too many exits and she had chosen the wrong one. Touma saw it before he could stop himself: the way her eyes slid to the wall, the way her mouth tightened around a joke that had not yet been made.

The safehouse was cheap in the way that made privacy a rumor. Thin walls. A desk that had been used for something else. A futon folded against the wall. An air conditioner that had gone quiet at the worst possible moment. The door stood open enough to show the hallway, and Nico stood at the edge of that hallway, a procedural buffer between the room and the rest of the safehouse, one hand near the doorframe, looking at the two of them as if they were a sentence he had not been trained to parse.

Touma understood the fear before he could name it. It was not that someone would see Mio. It was that someone would sort her. Classify her. Put her in a box with a label and a warning. The exchange between them had already become public, even with only Nico there, because the hallway was listening.

“If you’re going to explain this,” Mio said, “don’t phrase it like you’re removing my nervous system.”

“I’m not going to phrase it that way.”

“Everyone phrases it that way. ‘We need to remove the part that doesn’t fit.’ Very clean.”

Nico looked away first. He had the decency to do it slowly. He left the room because he was uncomfortable, but he did not slam the door. He simply stopped being in it, and the room became smaller. Not physically. The space he had occupied at the doorway seemed to close in, as if the room had been holding its breath and now had to decide what to do with the silence.

“Sit down,” Touma said. “There’s a chair.”

The chair by the desk was the only thing in the room that looked like it had been chosen by a person. The projector on the desk flickered, throwing a pale rectangle against the wall. Mio glanced at it.

“Is that thing haunted?” she asked.

“It’s a projector.”

“It’s trying to make sense of me.”

“No, it’s just a projector.”

“Then it’s doing a bad job.”

The quiet after the jokes was dangerous.

She sat.

The chair squeaked under her, a small, ordinary sound. For a second, Touma felt a relief so disproportionate it embarrassed him. The furniture had not rejected her. The room had not rejected her. The mundane world had made space, however briefly, for a person.

“Don’t make that face,” Mio said.

“What face?”

“The one that looks like you’re waiting for the chair to break.”

The fear was under the jokes. Touma could see it now: not the fear of pain, but the fear of being sorted. It looked like embarrassment, with a face.

“Can you eat?” he asked. “There’s something in the kitchen. Not much.”

Mio looked at him. “You’re asking if I can eat.”

“Yes.”

“That’s a very human question.”

“I’m trying.”

He realized he did not know the shape of her body in the way that mattered. He did not know what hurt, what was safe, what the limits were. He knew only that she was there, and that the unknowns had edges.

His hand moved halfway before he stopped it. He wanted to take her hand. He did not. She noticed. Her eyes flicked to his wrist, then back to his face, and something in her expression changed—not gratitude, exactly, but recognition.

Nico tried to leave the hallway. The first time, the corridor seemed to hold him. The second time, it let him go. He was gone, and the faint sound of the outer door closing reached them, but the room did not feel closed. It felt witnessed.

“I was going to ask if it hurt,” Touma said. “Before you noticed.”

The room did a strange half-glitch. The air conditioner stuttered. The projector’s beam thinned, then thickened. The light in the hallway outside the door seemed to lean a little too far into the room, as if the space were trying too hard to hold a shape that made sense.

Mio’s finger twitched. Just once. A small, involuntary thing, like a wire remembering it was a wire.

“Ground rules,” Touma said. “No surprise touching.”

Mio looked at him. “You’re making rules.”

“Tell me if something hurts.”

“I don’t want to be a problem.”

“People are problems. That’s the human part.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then: “If I say something is wrong, you will listen?”

Touma met her eyes. “If something is wrong, tell me. I’ll listen.”

Mio looked at him. “Okay.”

It was not trust. Not yet. But it was the first place trust could stand.

The door opened.

A support technician stood in the hallway, tablet in one hand, face arranged in the polite blankness of someone who had been trained to be useful and not to be present. It was the wrong kind of miracle: not rescue, but witness.

“Who is that?” the technician asked.

Mio went perfectly still.

Touma stood too fast. The chair gave a second squeak, sharper than the first.

“Not your business,” he said.

Embarrassment and fear arrived together, not as separate things but as one heat under his skin. He understood, with a sick clarity, that this was no longer a private moment. It was a story. The technician’s eyes had already begun to file it: the girl, the boy, the safehouse, the thing that was not in the file. Gossip had a shape, and it was forming.

“Leave,” Touma said.

The technician left, but the door stayed open a little too long. From the hallway, the technician said, “I’m not supposed to be here.”

That had been public. Visible. Humiliating. Not because anyone had shouted, but because someone had seen, and seeing had made it real. Privacy had been fragile, then broken, then false. There were too many eyes now, and it was too late for rumor to stay small.

“What do we do if people start asking?” Touma asked.

“We lie,” Mio said.

He felt disgust and affection at the same time, tangled together in his chest. “We lie,” he repeated. “Okay.”

They did not have a story yet. The lie would have to be built later, piece by piece, in the hallway or in the file or in the mouths of people who had already seen too much.

Mio’s voice was quiet, but it did not shake. “If I’m going to be seen, I refuse to be seen as a mistake.”

Touma heard the weight of it. It was not a request for comfort. It was a refusal.

“You’re not a mistake.”

The words were dangerous because they were true, and because he did not know how to prove them.

Mio laughed. It was small and shaky, more a crack than a sound. It did not cancel the fear. It sat beside it, like a hand on a cold window.

For one second, the room was bearable. The air conditioner hummed. The projector threw its pale rectangle against the wall. The door stood open, but the hallway did not seem to be listening. It was not safety. It was only a second, and it was enough to make the next second harder.

Then a soft scraping came from outside the room.

Not footsteps. Not the door.

It came from the wall near the bedroom door, low and slow, as if a fingertip were dragging along the other side of the plaster. The plaster seemed to take pressure. Not a crack, not a dent, but a change in the way the wall held itself, as if something on the other side were leaning.

A colder pulse ran through the limiter in Touma’s body, a warning that had no name. It was not fear, exactly. It was the body’s way of saying: something is being classified, and it is close.

The system was no longer just surveillance. It had a listening presence, and the presence was in the wall. The pressure was not just in the air. It was in the plaster, in the angle of the door, in the way the hallway seemed to lean toward the room. Touma did not know what the system was becoming. He only knew it was closer. It was not clear whether the sound was detection or something else. It had heard. It was leaning closer.

“Don’t let the system decide what I am,” Mio said.

The wall answered with a deeper scrape.
