

Mio looked away too quickly.

It was a small movement, the kind that could have been nothing. But Touma saw it before he could stop seeing it: her eyes sliding off his face, off the cheap safehouse bedroom, off the exposed doorway where the hallway light made a pale rectangle on the floor. The safehouse was cheap in the way that made every surface look temporary. The air conditioner was quiet. The futon edge was folded too neatly. The chair by the desk waited. The hallway edge was just beyond the doorway, and the wall near the bedroom door seemed closer than it had been a minute ago.

Privacy was failing in stages.

For a second, the room felt less like a room and more like a place where people might be classified.

He worried that people would classify her before they saw her as a person. The exchange they had just had was public in a way that was not normal. It had been phrased like a nervous-system removal: careful, clinical, and somehow intimate.

Nico stood first.

Nico had been a procedural buffer, a person who could stand in the doorway and make the moment less direct. Now Nico looked uncomfortable, decent enough to look away, and not built for this kind of intimacy. Nico left, becoming an absent witness.

After Nico left, the room became smaller.

The quiet after the jokes was dangerous.

Touma looked at the chair.

“Sit down,” he said.

The projector on the desk blinked.

“Haunted,” Mio said.

“No,” Touma said. “Just a projector.”

Mio looked at him, then at the chair. She sat.

The chair squeaked.

The squeak was acceptance. The chair held. The room was not rejecting her. That the furniture did not reject her mattered more than it should have, as if the mundane world were making space for her one ordinary sound at a time.

“Don’t make that face,” Mio said.

Touma touched his own face. “What face?”

Her fear looked like embarrassment, with a face. He recognized it under the jokes. It was not just embarrassment. It was fear wearing embarrassment because fear had nowhere else to go.

“Can you eat?” he asked.

The question came out too plainly. He wanted to be useful in a human way, without overstepping. He realized he had no map for her body. It was one of those body-shaped unknowns: not a secret, not a symptom, just a shape he did not know how to hold.

He wanted to take her hand.

He stopped.

He saw her notice that he was about to ask.

“I was going to ask if it hurt,” Touma said.

The room did a weird half-glitch thing. The light flickered. The air conditioner paused, then resumed, as if it were trying too hard to hold a shape that made sense.

Mio’s finger twitched.

“Ground rules,” Touma said.

Her finger twitched again.

“No surprise touching,” he said.

“And if something hurts?”

“If something hurts, tell me.”

“I don’t want to be a problem.”

“People are problems, humanly,” he said. “That doesn’t make you one.”

Mio looked at the chair. “If I say something is wrong, you will listen?”

“Yes.”

“Yes,” he said again, without performing comfort.

“I don’t trust you yet,” Mio said. “But I want to believe it’s possible.”

Trust was not yet belief.

Privacy had been fragile. Now it was broken. What remained was false.

The door opened.

A support technician stood in the doorway.

It was the wrong kind of miracle: a person who could help, and therefore could also tell.

“Who is that?” the support technician asked.

Mio was perfectly still.

Touma stood too fast.

“Not your business,” he said.

Embarrassment and fear arrived together on Mio’s face. He could see them both. His own embarrassment rose with them, and his dread of the public story climbed higher. He could also see the shift in the support technician’s eyes: concern becoming story, story becoming gossip.

“Leave,” Touma said.

The support technician left.

That was public. Visible. Humiliating.

Mio’s mouth opened.

“I’m not a mistake,” she said.

There were too many eyes now. It was too late for rumor to stay small.

“What do we do if people start asking?” Touma asked.

“We lie,” Mio said.

He looked at her. There was disgust in her expression, and under it, something like affection.

“If I am going to be seen,” Mio said, “I refuse to be seen as a mistake.”

“You are not a mistake,” Touma said.

It was a dangerous sincerity, the kind that could not be unsaid.

Mio laughed. It was small and shaky.

For one second, the room was bearable.

The warmth did not cancel the fear.

Then a soft scraping came from outside the room.

Not footsteps. Not the door.

A fingertip dragging on the other side of the wall.

Pressure in the plaster.

A colder limiter pulse, as a warning, touched the edge of Touma’s awareness. The system was not just surveillance now. It had a listening spatial presence. The classification pressure had become spatial. It had heard, and it was leaning closer.

“Don’t let the system decide what I am,” Mio said.

The wall answered with a deeper scrape.
