Chapter 5 — Visible Problem

Mio looked away too quickly.

Not the way someone changed the subject. The way someone moved their hand off a hot surface and then said it was fine.

Touma caught himself before the worry could settle into anything he'd have to act on. That was the problem with caring about a person who was actively trying not to be classified — you started classifying her yourself, sorting the flinches from the jokes from the silences, and then you were just another system with better intentions.

But the worry didn't leave. It just sat there, useless, the way worry did when it had no clearance for the job.

The exchange they'd just had wasn't normal. He knew that. She knew that. The room knew that, insofar as a cheap safehouse bedroom with a futon and a desk chair and an air conditioner ticking its quiet mechanical prayers could be said to know anything. What he didn't know was what to do about it — whether to acknowledge the abnormality and risk making it heavier, or to let it pass and risk making her think he hadn't noticed, which was its own kind of insult.

He went with the second one. Badly.

"So," he said, and the word came out like a man testing a floorboard. "That was — are you —"

He stopped. Tried again.

"Do you want to sit down?"

The question landed between them like something dropped. He watched her decide whether to pick it up.

Nico was still in the doorway. Touma could feel him there — not looking, very carefully not looking, the way a decent person didn't look at something they didn't have the context for and couldn't help with. Nico was uncomfortable in the specific way of someone who understood that what he was witnessing was private and also possibly important and that those two facts did not cancel each other out but did make his presence a problem.

"I should —" Nico started.

"Yeah," Touma said, before the sentence could become a plan.

Nico left. His footsteps went down the hallway and stopped, which meant he was standing at the far end trying to look like he'd had a reason to be there. Touma didn't blame him. Some rooms got smaller when a third person left, because the space they'd taken with them collapsed back. This one got smaller because what was left in it was too much for two people to pretend was casual.

Mio stood at the edge of the futon. Not sitting. Not leaving. The posture of someone who had been offered a surface and was checking whether it would hold a verdict about her.

"The chair's fine," Touma said. "It squeaks, but it's — it holds."

She looked at the chair the way someone looked at a door they'd been told was unlocked. Then she sat.

The chair squeaked. A small, cheap, ordinary sound — the complaint of a bolt that had been loose since before either of them had arrived. Mio's shoulders tightened for a fraction of a second, and then didn't.

Touma felt something in his chest release that he hadn't realized was clenched. The furniture hadn't rejected her. The room hadn't rejected her. A chair had squeaked and held, and that was enough — that was disproportionately, embarrassingly enough — to make the air in the room lose half a degree of its threat.

"Don't make that face," Mio said.

"What face?"

"The face where you're relieved the chair didn't throw me. I can see it. You look like someone whose dog just passed a test."

"The chair's not a dog."

"No, the chair's a chair. You're the one making it a referendum."

She was joking. Under the joke, the same way a wire ran under wallpaper, was something that wasn't joking. He could hear it — not because he was perceptive, but because the joke was too fast and too light and too exactly shaped to cover what was underneath. Fear looked like embarrassment when it had a face on. He was learning that. He was learning it the way you learned a language by being dropped in a country with no dictionary: too fast, too much, and every wrong word mattered.

"Can you eat?" he asked.

The question surprised both of them. He hadn't planned it. It had come from the part of his brain that had noticed she was standing differently — not weakly, but carefully, the way someone stood when they weren't sure their body was going to keep doing what they'd asked it to. He'd been looking at her without meaning to catalog what he was seeing, and what he'd seen was a person whose shape held unknowns he couldn't name.

He wanted to take her hand. The impulse arrived fully formed and he killed it before it reached his arm. No. Not now. Not without asking. Not without — she'd said nothing about touching and nothing about not touching and the absence of a rule was not permission.

She noticed. She noticed that he'd been about to do something and stopped, the way someone noticed a hand retracting from a doorbell.

"What were you going to say?" she asked.

"Nothing."

"You were going to ask if something hurt."

He was. He had been going to ask if it hurt, in the same stupid, clumsy, human way a person asked that question when they couldn't fix the thing that was hurting and needed to offer the only currency they had, which was acknowledgment. He closed his mouth.

The room did something.

It was small. A flicker — not in the lights, not in the air conditioner's rhythm, but in the quality of the space, as though the walls had flinched. The kind of half-glitch that could be a power fluctuation or a bad signal or nothing at all, and that you only noticed if you were already paying too much attention to the room because the person in it was paying too much attention to the room.

Touma's finger twitched. He felt it — a small involuntary pull, like something tugging at the nerve. He pressed his thumb against it and held it still.

Mio saw.

"Ground rules," she said. Not asking. Starting.

"Okay."

"No surprise touching. If you need to — if something — don't just reach for me. Tell me first."

"I wasn't going to —"

"I know. That's why I'm saying it now, while neither of us needs it, instead of later when one of us might."

He nodded. "No surprise touching. Got it."

"And if something hurts — with me, if I say something is wrong — you'll listen."

It wasn't a question. It was a test phrased as a statement, or a statement phrased as a test, and the difference between those two things was the entire distance between trust and the want to trust. She was standing at that distance and looking across it.

"Yes," he said. "If you say something is wrong, I listen. That's — yes."

She looked at him. Not searching. Not performing. Just looking, the way someone looked at a bridge before deciding whether to put weight on it.

"I don't want to be a problem," she said.

"People are problems. That's — that's what being a person is. You're a problem the way everyone's a problem. You're not a special category."

"That's the nicest insult anyone's ever given me."

"It wasn't —"

"I know. That's why it worked."

The room was bearable for one second. Not safe. Not comfortable. Not fixed. Bearable, the way a held breath was bearable — temporary, finite, and enough to get through the next thing.

Then the door opened.

The support tech stood in the hallway. Young, holding a tablet, wearing the slightly stunned expression of someone who had come to check a signal reading and walked into something they had no framework for.

"Oh," the tech said. "Sorry, I — the system flagged a —"

"Who is that?" Mio asked. She was perfectly still. Not frozen. Still, the way a person went still when they'd been seen and were deciding in the fraction of a second before the decision whether being seen was going to cost them something.

Touma stood too fast. The chair behind him scraped the floor.

"Not your business," he said. The words came out harder than he meant — sharper, more closed — because the fear had arrived at the same time as the anger and they'd used the same mouth. "We're — this isn't — you can't just walk in."

The tech's expression shifted. Touma watched it happen — the recognition, the recalculation, the moment where what they'd seen stopped being a scene and started being a story. A story they would tell. A story with the wrong shape and the wrong punchline and the wrong kind of miracle in it.

"Leave," Touma said.

The tech left. The door stayed open for a second too long, and then closed, and the hallway swallowed whatever was going to come of that.

Silence.

"That was public," Mio said. Her voice was flat. Not sarcastic. Not angry. Flat, the way a surface was flat after something had been scraped off it. "That was visible. That was — someone just saw me here, in this room, with you, and they're going to —"

"I know."

"Too many eyes. Too late for it to not be a rumor."

"I know."

"What do we do if people start asking?"

Touma looked at her. The disgust and the affection arrived together, the way they always did — disgust at the situation, at the system, at the tech's face doing that calculation in real time; affection for the person standing in front of him doing the math on how to survive being witnessed.

"We lie," he said.

Mio's mouth twitched. Not a smile. The ghost of one, the kind that haunted the place where a smile would be if the mouth trusted the room enough to let it through. "Of course we do."

A breath. Then, quieter, and with a weight that settled into the room like a stone dropped into still water:

"If I'm going to be seen, I refuse to be seen as a mistake."

The words were precise. Not rehearsed — precise the way things got precise when someone had thought about them for a long time and then said them once, exactly, because they'd only get to say them in a way that mattered once.

"You're not a mistake," Touma said.

He meant it. That was the dangerous part — not the words, which were ordinary, but the sincerity behind them, which was total and unguarded and the kind of thing that could be used against both of them if anyone with the right clearance heard it. He didn't care. He said it anyway, and the saying of it was the first thing he'd done all day that felt like it belonged to him instead of to the situation.

Mio looked at him. A small, shaky laugh came out of her — not humor, not relief, but the sound a person made when the room they were standing in became bearable for one second and they couldn't quite believe it and couldn't quite not believe it.

The room held. For one second, it held.

Then, from the other side of the bedroom wall, a sound.

Not footsteps. Not the door. Something slower, more deliberate — a soft scraping, like a fingertip dragging across plaster. It moved along the wall near the doorframe, steady, patient, the way something listened by touching.

Touma felt the pressure change in the room before he heard it properly. A shift in the air, a tightening, as though the wall had leaned closer by a fraction of an inch. The limiter pulse — the background signal he'd learned to stop noticing, the way you stopped noticing a clock — dropped half a degree colder. A warning, or something shaped like a warning.

"Don't let the system decide what I am," Mio said.
