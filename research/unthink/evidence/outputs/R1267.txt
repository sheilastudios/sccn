The ceiling was the same suspicious beige it had been every morning, and the carpet still felt like a rental. The broken lamp in the corner hadn't fixed itself. The room smelled of instant coffee, dust, and the cheap disinfectant that was supposed to make the place feel clean and instead made it feel explained.

None of that was the problem.

The problem was standing in the corner.

Not standing. Present. Occupying a volume of room that had been empty when he'd gone to sleep, in a way that was not voice, not pressure, not the private commentary he'd learned to live with — just there, with the blunt, stupid factuality of a chair that hadn't been there yesterday. A woman-shaped fact in a room that had stopped being private.

Touma stared.

His face was hot. His thoughts were doing the thing where they tried to reclassify something impossible as a livable inconvenience — okay, so this is happening now, this is a thing that happens, people wake up to— — and failing at it with the same energy as a man trying to fold a map in wind.

He blinked. She was still there.

He blinked again, deliberately, the way you'd test a monitor. Still there. Checking the room with her eyes, slow sweeps that tracked corners and doorframes and the shadow behind the kitchenette counter with the specific attention of someone looking for hidden teeth in a place that might bite.

She wasn't a voice anymore. She wasn't a pressure behind his thoughts or a commentary that arrived like weather. She was a person in his room, and the safe layer between strange and the world — the layer that had let him treat her like a feature of his own head, private, unregistered, his — was gone.

He reached for the blanket.

His hand went through her knee.

Not past it. Through it. The sensation was less like touching someone and more like putting your hand into a space that had forgotten to argue with you about whether it was solid. There was a lag — a half-second where his fingers expected resistance and found a kind of presence instead, not empty but not available, like static that had learned to keep still.

He yanked his hand back.

"Don't do that."

His own voice sounded like someone else's. Too loud for the room, too loud for six in the morning in a place with thin walls and a corridor that listened.

She looked at him. Her expression had the quality of someone who had already expected this reaction and was offended by it anyway — not at him, exactly, but at the situation, at the physics, at whatever rule of projection had made her knee a thing his hand could find without asking.

"I didn't mean to—"

"That's what makes it worse."

She was right. If he'd done it on purpose, it would have been a decision — stupid, maybe, but a decision. Instead his hand had reached for a blanket and found a person, and the accident was the part that made it unbearable, because it meant his body had already started treating her like she belonged in the geometry of the room. Like the space where his blanket was and the space where she was had become the same space, and his hand had simply gone where his hand went, and found something it had no framework for.

"Can you feel anything?" he asked, and then hated the question, because it was too broad and too close to the kind of question you'd ask a person, not a projection, not a presence, not a—

"Physically or emotionally?"

She said it flat. Not a joke. Not quite not a joke. The kind of answer that understood his question better than he'd meant it and made him pay for the precision.

He looked at her. She looked back. And the thing that made his chest tighten wasn't that she was impossible. It was that she was more than she'd been. More than the voice. More than the pressure. The audible Mio — the one who'd lived in his head like a tenant with strong opinions and no lease — had been vivid, sharp, real in the way that things are real when they have no choice. This Mio had shoulders. The shoulders were tight. She was deciding where to put her hands — at her sides, crossed, one on the doorframe — and the deciding was visible, and the visibility made it a kind of honesty the voice had never had to manage.

He could see her fear.

Not dramatic. Not cinematic. Just there, in the way she held herself slightly too still, in the way her eyes kept checking the room's exits, in the breath she took before answering that was one count longer than it needed to be. The audible Mio had been afraid plenty of times. He'd never been able to see it before. It had been a fact he'd taken on trust, the way you trust someone's tone of voice. Now it was a fact he could watch happen, and watching it made it a different kind of fact — heavier, less negotiable, harder to file under manageable.

She paced. Three steps one way, three steps back, the narrow strip between the futon and the kitchenette counter, and his eyes followed her the way eyes follow the only moving thing in a room. His body kept treating her like the center of it. Not a choice. Not a decision. Just the gravity of a visible person doing a visible thing in a space that had been empty of visible people, and his attention rearranging itself around her like furniture pushed to the walls.

He kept glancing at her. He couldn't stop. It was the same impulse as checking a stove — not because he doubted it, but because the fact of it kept needing to be reconfirmed. She was there. She was still there. She was pacing, and her pacing had weight, and the weight was the problem, because weight meant here, and here meant registered, and registered meant—

The door opened.

Nico stood in the doorway. He looked at Touma on the futon. He looked at Mio in the strip between the counter and the bed. His face did something complicated and not entirely successful — an attempt at calm that arrived as the specific stillness of a man who had walked into a room expecting one problem and found two.

"You're both awake."

"You knew she could do that," Touma said, and it wasn't a question, but it wanted to be.

"No." Nico's eyes stayed on Mio. "Not this. Not — this fast."

Mio stopped pacing. She stood with her weight on one foot, the other slightly raised, as if the floor had asked her a question she hadn't finished answering. She looked at Nico the way someone looks at a doctor who's about to say something in a tone designed to be reassuring.

"So this is the first problem," she said. "The first one outside the closed system. The one that talks back."

Nico didn't correct her. He was looking at her the way you look at a fire that has moved from the fireplace to the curtain — not with panic, but with the specific recalibration of a person whose list of problems has just been reorganized by category.

"The visible rule still applies," he said, to both of them, to the doorway, to the room. "You're in the witness range. Anyone in the room can see you. That part hasn't changed."

"Hasn't changed," Touma repeated. "Just expanded."

"The limiter's stable for the moment. The projection isn't." Nico ran a hand through his hair, and the gesture was too fast, too ordinary, too much like a man doing a thing with his hands because his hands needed something to do. "This isn't a school presentation. This isn't — she's not standing at the front of the room with slides."

"No," Mio said. "I'm standing in the back of the room with you."

Nico looked at her. Something in his expression shifted — not softened, exactly, but adjusted, the way a lens adjusts. "She takes the side where we continue existing. That's the argument."

"It's not an argument. It's a side effect."

"Of what?"

"Of being a person. You wanted precision. That's the precision."

The room held the three of them in the specific silence of people who have arrived at the edge of what they know and are looking at what they don't. Nico's hand dropped from his hair. He looked at the doorframe, then at the ceiling, then at the place where the broken lamp should have been working.

"The other systems," he said. "If she's visible — if this is visible, not just to us, not just in this room—"

"Cameras," Touma said.

"Biometrics." Nico's voice had gone flat, procedural, the voice of a man reading from a list he didn't want to be holding. "Recognition filters. If any of the building's systems are running face match, gait analysis, presence detection — if there's a camera in the hall with a live feed—"

"Or a person," Mio said.

Nico looked at her.

"A person," he repeated. "Walking past. Looking through a door left open too long. A clerk at the front desk who remembers a face that wasn't on the register."

The cheap carpet held the silence. The beige ceiling offered no comment. The broken lamp, characteristically, declined to help.

Touma looked at Mio, who stood in the narrow strip between his futon and the counter, visible, afraid, composed, and more present than she had ever been when she'd been only a voice in his head — present enough to be seen by a camera, a system, a stranger in a hallway, present enough to be a problem that didn't end at the door.

"Bad enough for you?" she asked.

It wasn't a joke. It wasn't not a joke. It was the question of a person who had just learned she could be perceived and was already doing the arithmetic on what it would cost.
