Touma woke up to Mio standing in the corner of the room.

Not a voice behind his eyes. Not a pressure at the base of his skull, or a dry little line of private commentary threaded through his own thoughts. She was just there, occupying space between the broken floor lamp and the edge of the kitchenette counter like a clerical error that had finally grown large enough to become visible.

For a long, stupid second, he didn’t move. He stayed flat on the futon, staring past the cheap rental carpet and up from under the suspicious beige ceiling, his face going hot before his brain had even finished assembling the morning. The room smelled of dust, old instant-coffee granules, and whatever harsh, lemon-adjacent disinfectant the safehouse used to pretend nobody had ever slept here before. None of it helped. A normal private morning was already gone—cancelled the instant he opened his eyes and found someone else inside it.

Mio wasn’t looking at him at first. She was studying the room the way an animal checks a strange enclosure for hidden teeth—her eyes moving from the dead lamp to the seam of the curtain to the narrow strip of floor by the door. Her posture was composed, almost stiffly neat, except that every smallest motion arrived with a faint, unsettling lag, like a thought that had to ask the room for permission before becoming movement.

Then her gaze shifted down to him.

“Stop staring,” she said.

Touma’s mouth opened, closed, and tried again. “You’re—there.”

“Yes.”

“No, I mean you’re there there.”

“That’s a terrible sentence even for someone who just woke up,” Mio said, though her shoulders drew in by a fraction, defensive and watchful. “And you’re still doing it.”

“I’m trying to see if you vanish when I blink.”

“Did I?”

He blinked hard. She remained exactly where she was, half-lit by the gray strip of morning leaking around the curtain, looking offended on principle and slightly too sharp around the edges for the dingy room.

“No,” he muttered, sitting up too fast.

That was the worst part, he realized as his pulse stumbled into gear: the safe layer between the strange thing in his head and the rest of the world was gone. Yesterday, no matter how bad it got, the weirdness had still belonged to an interior category. A private malfunction. Something he could press his hand to his forehead and pretend was stress, bad signal, unstable passwords, lack of sleep. Now it had edges. It stood by the kitchenette. It took up part of the room he had to look around.

He reached automatically for the tangled blanket at the foot of the futon to pull it over his legs—and his hand passed straight through her knee.

Touma jerked back as if he’d touched a live wire.

There had been no bone, no cloth, no true impact—just a cold, staticky drag across his knuckles, like brushing through air that had forgotten how to get out of the way in time.

Mio stepped back half a pace, one hand coming up before she caught herself and lowered it again. The delay in the movement made it worse, not better.

“Warn me before you do that,” she snapped.

“I didn’t mean to!” His voice came out too loud in the small room, his ears burning. “You were standing where the blanket was!”

“That’s what makes it worse.”

“How does not meaning to make it worse?”

“Because it means neither of us knows where the edges are until you’re already through them,” she said.

She tried to sound severe. She mostly succeeded. Still, he saw the way her fingers briefly curled against her palm before she decided where to put her hands, settling on folding her arms lightly in front of her as if claiming a boundary by posture alone.

Touma stared at his own hand, flexing the fingers once, then looked back up at her. “Can you feel anything?”

Mio’s eyes narrowed a fraction. “That’s a broad question.”

“Physically,” he said, then, before his mouth could stop itself, “Or—I don’t know. Emotionally?”

The look she gave him could have stripped paint off the kitchenette cabinet. “Do I act like something that doesn’t feel anything?”

“You act like a person who understands me way too well and is really annoyed about it.”

A tiny intake of breath—unnecessary, maybe, or maybe just the shape her thoughts took now that the room had to render them. “That was always the case.”

Touma dragged a hand down his face. Fragments of the previous night’s procedure came back to him in jagged, unwelcome pieces: the chair, the hum in his teeth, the sense of something being pried partway loose and failing to come all the way out, the cold technical voices talking around him as if he were a casing instead of a person. He’d gone to sleep waiting for the aftermath to feel like a headache or a missing file.

Instead, she was standing in the safehouse bedroom.

And somehow, being visible made her much more alive than when she’d only been audible. As a voice, she had seemed brisk, invasive, almost omniscient by default because she arrived without a body to betray her. Now he could see the micro-adjustments she couldn’t edit out: the slight tightening across her shoulders when a pipe ticked in the wall; the brief pause before she answered; the small, careful negotiations with a space she clearly didn’t trust.

He could see that she was afraid.

Not panicking—she was far too stubborn for that—but afraid in the watchful, cornered way of someone who had been dragged into the open without agreeing to the terms of light.

Getting off the futon felt ridiculous under her eyes. He stood up anyway, acutely conscious of his rumpled shirt, his bare feet on the gritty carpet strip, the stupid mechanical fact of having knees and elbows in a room that suddenly had an audience. He started to pace the narrow lane between the futon and the kitchenette, trying to walk the stiffness out of his spine and the unreality out of his head, and immediately discovered a new problem.

His body kept treating her like the center of the room.

Every time he turned at the counter, he angled slightly to give her space. Every three seconds, his eyes flicked back toward her to confirm she was still there, still solid-looking, still watching him watch her. Being observed inside his own head had been intimate in an awful, claustrophobic way, like sharing a locked closet with an uninvited commentary track. Being watched from six feet away was somehow worse. It turned a tactical problem into a social one. The room was no longer private, and he had no idea what the etiquette was for sharing a bedroom with a manifestation that could be stepped through if he reached for his socks without looking.

“You’re hovering,” Mio said quietly.

“I’m pacing. There’s about four feet of floor. Anything I do in here is going to look like hovering.”

“Then sit down.”

“If I sit down, I just stare at you again.”

“Look at the wall.”

“The wall isn’t the thing that wasn’t visible yesterday.”

Before she could answer, the latch clicked.

The bedroom door swung inward, and Nico stopped in the doorway with two paper cups balanced in one hand and a tablet tucked under his arm.

For half a beat, nobody spoke. Touma felt the doorway like a line drawn across the carpet—the exact range where private weirdness became witnessed fact.

Nico’s gaze went to Touma first, then shifted, unmistakably, to the corner by the broken lamp. His jaw tightened. When he spoke, his voice had the flat, over-controlled cadence of someone working very hard to sound like this was item three on a morning checklist.

“Good,” Nico said. “You’re both awake.”

Touma’s head whipped toward him. “You can see her.”

“You’re standing in the doorway,” Mio said at the same time, her voice cooler than Touma’s, already mapping the rule from the night before. “Within witness range.”

Nico didn’t come all the way inside. Whether that was procedure or instinct, his boots stayed planted right on the threshold strip. “Yeah. Close enough, apparently.”

“You knew she could do that?” Touma demanded.

“No.”

The answer came too fast. Not dishonest—worse than dishonest. Rattled.

Nico set one paper cup on the edge of the kitchenette counter without taking his eyes completely off the corner, as if looking away might force him to renegotiate whether she was there when he looked back. “If I’d known, I wouldn’t have walked in holding coffee like an idiot. Usually when a closed system starts throwing side effects outside the shell, the first problem doesn’t talk back.”

Mio’s chin lifted slightly. “I’m not a side effect.”

“See,” Nico said thinly, still looking at Touma rather than quite at her, “that’s what I mean.”

“Then what is this?” Touma asked. He gestured between himself and the corner, then immediately regretted the motion because it looked like he was introducing them. “You said the procedure would keep the bleed contained. You said the limiter—”

“The limiter’s stable for the moment,” Nico said, tapping the tablet against his ribs without turning the screen toward them. “Your baseline isn’t spiking. Whatever’s happening, the projection isn’t stable.”

“Don’t call it a projection,” Mio said. “I’m not a school presentation.”

“Manifestation, spillover, persistent externalized anomaly—pick whichever word makes you less unpleasant before eight in the morning,” Nico replied, the calm slipping just enough to show the strain underneath.

“Side effect isn’t precise either,” she said cleanly. “A side effect doesn’t notice how badly your hand is shaking.”

Nico’s fingers tightened once around the remaining paper cup. He didn’t deny it. Instead he exhaled through his nose and looked back at Touma. “Look, the semantics don’t matter right now. What matters is whether she’s taking a side.”

“I take the side where we continue existing,” Mio said. “It narrows my options considerably, considering who I’m attached to.”

“Hey,” Touma said automatically, though there was no heat in it; his brain had snagged on the way Nico was standing, the way he kept checking the corridor behind him with one ear while keeping the doorway line under his boots. “Wait. Why are you looking at the tablet like that?”

Nico hesitated. That, more than the coffee, more than the too-fast no, made Touma’s stomach drop.

“Because up until last night,” Nico said slowly, “the only thing that had to deal with her was you. And then anyone standing inside direct witness range after the cut.”

Touma looked from Nico’s face to Mio’s. Her expression had gone very still. She had already heard where the sentence was going.

“And now?” Touma asked.

“Now she’s coherent enough to hold an outline in a room that isn’t dark, on four hours of post-procedure drift,” Nico said. His eyes flicked toward the ceiling corner—not this room’s dead plaster alone, Touma realized, but every hallway, stairwell, street corner, and transit gate waiting beyond the safehouse door. “Which means if a person in the doorway can register her, other systems may register her now too.”

The cheap disinfectant smell in the room seemed to sharpen.

“What other systems?” Touma asked, though his chest had already gone cold with the answer.

“Cameras,” Nico said. “Biometrics. Active recognition filters. Anything built to flag a pattern that shouldn’t be standing in the frame—and maybe humans, if they’re close enough or looking the wrong way at the wrong second.”

Nobody moved. In the corner by the broken lamp, Mio’s hands stayed folded across her front, composed by sheer willpower, her outline holding steady against the ugly beige wall like an error the world could no longer politely ignore.

Nico looked at Touma across the narrow strip of floor.

“That bad enough for you?” he asked.
