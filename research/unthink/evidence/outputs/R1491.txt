Mio looked away too quickly.

Not the way someone does when they're done with a conversation. The way someone does when they've calculated exactly how many seconds of eye contact will read as normal and then subtracted one for safety. Touma caught it and filed it where he filed most things about Mio — in the space between I noticed and I will not be the person who makes it worse by noticing out loud.

The worry wasn't that he couldn't see her. He could see her fine. The worry was that other people would classify her before they ever got that far. That she'd become a category in someone's taxonomy before she became a face, and the face wouldn't matter anymore.

Nico was still in the room, which meant the exchange between them wasn't private. It wasn't a normal conversation between two people in a room. It was a performance with an audience of one who hadn't asked for a ticket, and the audience was uncomfortable enough to look at the floor, which was decent of him, and not built for this, which was also true.

"Right," Nico said, with the specific tone of a person who had decided that leaving was the kindest thing he was capable of. "I'll be — yeah."

He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to. The room got smaller when he left, not larger, which was the opposite of what physics promised and exactly what privacy actually did when it arrived in stages instead of all at once. The door closed behind him with the soft, final sound of someone removing themselves from a situation they would describe later as fine, mostly, I think.

Touma sat down on the futon edge. The frame protested. He stayed.

Mio stood in the doorway for a beat too long — not dramatic, just the pause of someone checking whether the room would tolerate her before she committed to it. Then she crossed to the desk chair and sat.

The chair squeaked.

Touma felt a relief he was embarrassed to feel. The furniture had not rejected her. A chair had accepted her weight with an ordinary, cheap, mechanical sound, and that mundane fact mattered more to him than it should have. The world was making space. A squeak was not a welcome, but it was not a refusal, and right now the distance between those two things was the width of everything.

"Don't make that face," Mio said.

"What face."

"The one where you look like I just passed a test the chair didn't know it was giving."

He almost smiled. He didn't, because underneath the sarcasm he could hear the thing she was doing with it — the thing she always did, which was build a floor out of jokes so the fear underneath had somewhere to stand that wasn't her face. The fear looked like embarrassment when it wore her expressions. It wasn't embarrassment. It was the fear of being seen as something that needed to be explained, and the jokes were the explanation she offered first, before anyone else could offer a worse one.

"Can you eat?" he asked.

The question came out before he'd finished thinking it, which was the only way it could have come out honestly. She looked at him, and for a half-second the sarcasm dropped and what was underneath was not vulnerability — it was the shape of a body full of things he didn't have names for, unknowns that had nothing to do with personality and everything to do with what she was now, physically, in ways that neither of them could chart on a diagram.

He had wanted to take her hand. The impulse had arrived in his wrist before it arrived in his mind, and he'd stopped it — not because he didn't want to, but because touching her without asking would make him exactly the kind of person she was afraid of, the kind who treated her body as a thing that could be reached for.

She noticed. She noticed that he had been about to ask something, or reach for something, and had stopped. Her eyes tracked the small interruption in his posture the way someone tracks a sound they can't identify — not suspicious, just alert, the way you stay alert in a room that has been unsafe before.

"I was going to ask if it hurt," he said.

"It always hurts."

That was not an answer to what he'd asked. It was an answer to what he'd meant, which was close enough to honesty that it landed.

The room did something.

Not a glitch, exactly. The air conditioner cycled, and the light shifted, and for a fraction of a second the walls seemed to hold their shape the way a person holds a smile they're not sure of — with effort, and not quite convincingly. As though the room was trying too hard to be a room, to hold a form that made sense, and the effort showed.

Mio's finger twitched. She noticed him noticing, and her jaw tightened.

"Ground rules," she said.

He waited.

"No surprise touching. None. If you need to touch me, you say it first and I say yes or no, and if I say no, that's the end."

"Agreed."

"Tell me if something hurts. Not if it's wrong — if it hurts. Wrong is a word for systems. I'm not a system."

"Agreed."

"I don't want to be a problem."

"People are problems. That's the human version."

She looked at him. Not the quick, calculated look from before. A longer one, the kind that happened when someone was deciding whether to believe a thing they wanted to believe and couldn't tell yet if wanting was enough.

"If I say something is wrong," she said, "you'll listen."

"Yes."

Not performed. Not said with the warmth of someone offering comfort. Just said, the way you say a thing you mean and then stop talking because adding more would make it less true.

She didn't say I trust you. What she did was stop performing the absence of trust, which was different, and quieter, and the closest thing to a beginning that either of them had.

The door opened.

The support tech stood in the hallway with the expression of a person who had come to check on something routine and found something that was not routine, that was not in any manual, that was the wrong kind of miracle — the kind that doesn't inspire awe but gossip, the kind that walks into a bar later and becomes a story told to someone who tells someone who tells someone.

"Who is that?" the tech said, looking at Mio.

Mio went perfectly still. Not the stillness of someone at rest. The stillness of someone who has learned that motion attracts classification, and stillness is the only camouflage that works when you can't leave.

Touma stood too fast.

"Not your business."

It came out harder than he meant it, which was also the right amount, because the tech's face was already doing the thing — the recognition shift, the I'm going to tell someone about this shift, the moment where a person stops seeing a person and starts seeing a story they get to be the first to tell.

"Leave," Touma said.

The tech left. The door closed again, but the privacy it restored was the false kind — the kind that exists only because the witness is temporarily out of sight, not because the witnessing has stopped.

"That was public," Mio said. Her voice was flat. "That was visible. That was humiliating."

"I know."

"Don't — " She stopped. Started again, with the precision of someone choosing words the way you choose footing on a ledge. "If I am going to be seen, I refuse to be seen as a mistake."

The line landed harder than she'd probably intended. It wasn't a self-esteem statement. It wasn't I deserve better. It was a refusal — explicit, structural, the kind of defiance that happens when someone has run out of room to be private and decides that if the world is going to look, it will look at a person, not an anomaly, not a symptom, not a case file.

"You're not a mistake," Touma said.

He meant it. That was the dangerous part — sincerity in a room this fragile, with this many cracks, where the wrong weight in the wrong place could bring the ceiling down. He meant it, and he said it simply, and the simplicity was the only thing that kept it from being too much.

Mio made a small, shaky sound. A laugh, maybe. Or the place where a laugh would go if it could afford to arrive. It didn't cancel the fear. It sat next to it, the way a candle sits next to a wall — small, warm, not enough, but there.

For one second, the room was bearable.

Then something scraped against the wall outside the bedroom door.

Not footsteps. Not a knock. Not the sound a door makes when someone leans on it. Something slower. A fingertip dragging across the other side of the plaster, the way a person traces a line they're reading — except no one was reading, and the line had no text, and the pressure behind it was not casual.

The plaster dimpled. Not much. Enough to see if you were looking, and Touma was looking.

The limiter pulsed. A cold warning, the system's way of saying I am aware of this — except it wasn't saying it to them. It was saying it to itself, and they were the ones who heard it, and hearing it was not the same as being told.

"Don't let the system decide what I am," Mio said.

The wall answered with a deeper scrape.
