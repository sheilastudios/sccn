The headache was already blooming behind Touma’s eyes before he even reached the bottom of the stairs. That was the first warning.

The second warning followed a breath later: the old transit station took on extra edges. The fluorescent tubes overhead burned too sharp against the cracked tile, the hum of the row of vending machines vibrated too loud in his teeth, and the evening commuters began to curve their paths with that practiced, eyes-forward noninvolvement of strangers who had decided not to see something. You could outrun trouble when it stayed outside in the corridor. Trouble inside your own skull was a different matter.

Touma kept walking, tired to the bone, letting the ache guide him toward the service gate at the end of the platform.

A man in a gray coat stood by the metal door, his posture brisk and procedural, as if he were merely filing paperwork. One hand was wrapped tight around the wrist of a small-built teenager in a crumpled uniform, a heavy school bag slipping off the boy’s shoulder. The man’s other hand stayed tucked neatly inside his coat. The boy wasn't shouting or pulling back; his face was slack with blank, breathless terror.

Touma stopped. What pulled him forward wasn't gallantry. Touma had aged out of gallantry a long time ago. It was recognition.

He closed the distance to six steps.

"Hey," Touma said.

The man in the gray coat didn't even turn his head, his mind already narrowing around a single, clean directive: keep moving, keep the kid quiet, finish the handoff.

Touma reached out and slid a paperknife to the edge of the thought.

It felt like pushing a pin straight through the inside of the world. He didn't shatter the man's mind—he simply unthought the bridge between intention and muscle. The man’s grip slacked at once. His knees unlatched, and he folded down against the base of the service gate as if keeping a sudden, private appointment with gravity.

The boy stumbled backward, his school bag thudding against his hip, chest heaving as he looked up at Touma. For half a second, the terror cleared from his eyes, replaced by a flash of pure, startled relief—the look of someone who knew exactly who had just called out to him.

Then the second half arrived.

A faint, invisible hiccup passed through the air between them, the price of the cut settling its tab. The boy blinked hard, his hand rising to his temple. When his eyes found Touma again, the warmth had been wiped clean out of them, replaced by the guarded confusion of a kid looking at a total stranger in a dangerous place. It was a clean, quiet cruelty, and there was nothing Touma could do but stand there and let the boy back away.
