# 05 — Visible Problem

Touma had imagined a lot of bad mornings in his life.

He had imagined waking up to a broken window.
He had imagined waking up to police outside.
He had imagined waking up to the sort of silence that meant somebody had already made a decision about him.

He had not imagined waking up to Mio standing in the corner of his room with her arms folded and her expression exactly as offended as it had always been, because that would have required him to admit, in advance, that there were worse things than her voice in his head.

She was there anyway.

Not a voice.
Not a pressure at the base of his skull.
Not a hiss of commentary timed to ruin his dignity.

A girl-shaped hologram, pale and slightly too bright at the edges, standing on the cheap rental carpet of the safehouse bedroom like the world had finally made a clerical error large enough to become visible.

Touma sat up so fast he banged his elbow against the wall.

“Don’t do that,” he muttered.

Mio tilted her head. “Do what?”

“Be there.”

“That is not a realistic request.”

He groaned and flopped back onto the futon. The ceiling was still the same suspicious beige. The lamp was still broken. The safehouse still smelled faintly like dust, instant coffee, and the cheap disinfectant Nico had used to make the place feel medically secure without actually improving anything.

Mio looked around the room with the intense suspicion of someone checking a trap for hidden teeth.

Touma watched her do it and had the deeply unhelpful thought that she looked more alive than she had when she was only audible.

That thought made his face hot.

“Stop staring,” she said.

“I’m not staring.”

“You are absolutely staring.”

“I’m trying to figure out if you’ll vanish if I blink.”

Mio gave him a flat look. “If I do, it will be because I finally developed taste.”

Touma huffed a laugh before he could stop himself.

The laugh made the whole room feel less stable.

That was the problem now. There was no longer any safe layer between the strange part of his life and the rest of it. The thing that had once lived inside his head and insulted him in private now occupied space. Space had rules. Space had witnesses. Space had consequences.

And Touma, who had spent most of his life hiding his weirdness behind being difficult and unapproachable, had discovered that the universe could be much crueler than social embarrassment if it wanted to be.

He reached for the blanket. His hand passed through her knee by accident when he moved too quickly.

Mio jerked back. “Warn me before you do that.”

Touma froze. “I didn’t mean to.”

“That’s what makes it worse.”

He stared at her.

Then she looked away first, which was somehow more alarming than if she had kept glaring.

Touma swung his legs over the side of the futon and sat up. “Can you feel anything?”

Mio’s eyes narrowed. “That is a broad question.”

“Don’t start.”

“I’m trying to decide whether you mean physically or emotionally.”

Touma buried his face in his hands. “I hate that you can do that now.”

“Do what?”

“Act like a person who understands me too well.”

A pause.

Mio’s expression shifted in the smallest way. Not quite softer. Not quite sad. Something more guarded than either.

“That was always the case,” she said.

Touma lowered his hands.

He wanted to argue with her. It would have been easier to argue.

Instead, because his life apparently hated ease, the memory of the procedure came back in pieces: Nico’s voice, the ring of monitors, the cold pressure behind his skull when the limiter began to separate, and the moment the room had not just seen Mio but accepted that it had to do something with her.

He rubbed at the back of his neck.

“You know,” he said, “I was expecting ‘death’ or ‘explosion.’ Not this.”

“This,” Mio said, looking down at her own hand, “is also objectionable.”

She flexed her fingers once.

Touma watched the movement with a stupid, helpless amount of focus.

There was a faint lag between intention and result, like a thought that had to ask the room for permission before becoming motion. The projection caught the light in soft bands, not quite transparent, not quite solid, and every time she shifted her weight the outline of her body shimmered at the edges in a way that made Touma think of bad signal and unstable passwords.

He hated that he could see her fear now.

Not because it made him dislike her.

Because it meant he could no longer pretend he had only been dealing with a voice.

Voices could be snide, invasive, annoying, and still remain safely abstract if you wanted them to. A visible Mio had shoulders that tightened when something unexpected happened. A visible Mio took a breath before answering. A visible Mio had to decide where to put her hands when the room got crowded.

A visible Mio could be ignored.

That thought made him angry enough to stand up.

Mio immediately lifted her chin. “If you are about to blame me for physics, I will be very disappointed.”

“I’m not blaming you.”

“Good.”

“I’m blaming the universe.”

“Reasonable.”

Touma ran a hand through his hair and started pacing in the small strip of floor between the futon and the kitchenette. The safehouse had been designed for short-term concealment, which apparently meant it contained one bedroom, a single narrow window, and exactly enough bad furniture to create ankle hazards in dim light.

Mio watched him pace.

He could feel it.

Being watched by her used to mean feeling judged in the privacy of his own skull. Being watched by her now meant he kept accidentally glancing toward her because his body had not yet learned how to stop treating her like the center of the room.

That was a problem.

Not a tactical one.

A social one.

Which, unfortunately, was somehow worse.

The door opened.

Touma jerked so hard he nearly hit the kitchenette counter.

Nico leaned in with a tablet in one hand and the look of a man who had spent the last hour deciding whether this counted as a miracle, a disaster, or a lawsuit. “You’re both awake.”

Touma stared at him. “You knew she could do that?”

“No.”

“That sounded too fast.”

“That is because the answer is no and I am tired.”

Mio folded her arms. “You are always tired.”

Nico looked directly at her. “You are the first problem in this room that has ever talked back at me from outside a closed system.”

Mio raised one eyebrow. “Then you have lived a sheltered life.”

Touma covered his face with one hand.

Nico stepped fully into the room and, very carefully, did not react to the fact that he was now sharing space with Mio as though that were the most normal thing in the world. He was trying too hard to be calm, which meant he was absolutely not calm.

Touma had never seen him look this close to being rattled.

That made Touma feel marginally better.

Then Nico’s eyes flicked once, not quite to Mio and not quite past her, and Touma remembered the stupid rule from yesterday—the one that said they had to stay visible, confirmed, observed, because apparently even being weird now came with a timer.

So of course Nico was standing in the doorway like he was trying not to let the room drift out of witness range.

Great. Even the embarrassment had operational procedure.

Nico held up the tablet. “The limiter is stable. For the moment. That doesn’t mean the projection is stable.”

“Projection,” Touma repeated.

Mio’s eyes narrowed. “Do not call me that like I’m a school presentation.”

Nico ignored that. “Your system is still interpreting her as part of the same control architecture. The visible form is a side effect.”

“Side effect,” Touma echoed. “You say that like you’re not looking at a person.”

Nico’s expression sharpened. “I am looking at a person. That’s why I’m being precise.”

Touma didn’t like that answer, which was probably because it sounded more honest than comforting.

Mio’s gaze flicked from Nico to Touma. “He’s right.”

Touma stared at her. “You don’t get to take his side.”

“I didn’t take his side. I took the side where we continue existing.”

“That’s not comforting either.”

“It wasn’t meant to be.”

Nico exhaled through his nose and tapped the tablet. “The important part is that this means other systems may be able to register her now.”

Touma froze.

Mio’s expression did not change, but her shoulders went tight.

“Other systems,” Touma said carefully, “as in people?”

“As in cameras, biometric scanners, active recognition filters, maybe some humans if the conditions are bad enough.”

“Bad enough for who?”

Nico gave him a flat look.

Touma realized the answer before it came.

“For you.”

That was a very rude way to wake up.
