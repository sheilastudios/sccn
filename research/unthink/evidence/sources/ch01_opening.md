# 01 — The Girl in the Failsafe

By the time Touma noticed the man in the gray coat had a hand around the boy's wrist, the headache was already blooming behind his eyes.

That was the first warning.

The second was the way the world acquired extra edges: platform lights too sharp, vending-machine hum too loud, every stranger in the old transit station pretending not to notice anything with the practiced concentration of people who wanted trouble to choose someone else.

Touma had learned, very early in his life, that some kinds of trouble could not be outrun. A bad grade, yes. A teacher with the patience of a broken appliance, probably. A man with a knife, if you had a good alley and bad luck.

But the kind already inside your skull?

That one walked after you.

So Touma swore, stopped thinking too hard about what he was doing, and moved.

The recruiter was at the far end of the platform, hand tucked inside his coat, shoulders too calm for a man who had just taken a child by the wrist and tried to steer him toward a service gate.

Not a child. A small-built teen with a school bag and the same too-controlled stillness Touma had spent years seeing in reflective surfaces and train windows. Touma had caught the arm at the wrong second and seen the teenager’s eyes go blank with the particular terror of someone deciding that resisting might make things worse.

That blankness was what made Touma move.
It was not gallantry. It was recognition.

He crossed the distance in six steps.

“Hey,” he said.

The recruiter looked up, already annoyed, as if Touma had interrupted a private appointment with gravity itself.

Touma met his eyes and reached for the thought that was forming behind them.

Not the man’s whole mind. Not some grand fantasy of domination. Just the one ugly, precise idea that was guiding the hand in the coat: keep moving, keep the kid quiet, finish the handoff.

Unthink.

The word didn’t come out loud. It never did. It hit like a pin pushed through the inside of the world.

The recruiter blinked once.

His hand came out of the coat empty.

The teen stumbled back so hard he nearly fell over the yellow line.

For half a second, Touma felt the familiar relief: it worked.

Then the second half arrived.

Touma’s own memory hiccupped.

Not a dramatic blackout. Not a theatrical collapse. Just a tiny, mean absence, as if someone had taken a paperknife to the edge of a thought and left the rest of it standing there confused.

He knew he’d moved. He knew he’d used Unthink. He knew the man had been about to do something bad.

What he did not know, suddenly, was why the kid looked so familiar.

That part vanished with a clean, quiet cruelty.
