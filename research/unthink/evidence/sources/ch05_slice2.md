He looked at Mio, and she looked away too quickly.

Touma’s stomach sank.

“Wait,” he said. “You’re worried about people seeing her.”

“I’m worried about people classifying her,” Nico said. “Seeing is the harmless version.”

Mio made a small, almost offended noise. “Harmless is an ambitious word for this group.”

Nico nodded once. “Exactly. Which is why I need both of you to understand that you cannot act normally until we know what the projection is leaking.”

Touma barked a laugh. “Normally? We’re past that by several crimes.”

Mio looked at him. “You say that like you didn’t try to remove me from your nervous system less than an hour ago.”

Touma pointed at her. “Do not phrase it like that in front of him.”

Nico, infuriatingly, did not blink. “I already knew.”

“That is not the point.”

“It’s a point.”

Touma made a noise of despair and sat down hard on the edge of the futon.

The mattress creaked under his weight.

Mio’s eyes tracked the movement, then the exact spot where he had sat, and Touma had the sudden terrible realization that she was worried about whether she could share space without being treated as a visual anomaly.

That thought hit him harder than the embarrassment.

He looked up at her. “Sit down.”

Mio blinked. “Excuse me?”

“Sit. If you’re going to stand there looking like a haunted projector, at least use the chair.”

“I do not look haunted.”

“You absolutely do.”

She glared at him, then at the chair, then at Nico as if daring him to comment.

Nico, wisely, pretended not to exist.

Mio took one careful step and sat on the chair by the desk.

The chair made a tiny squeak.

Touma watched her settle and felt a completely unjustified surge of relief that the furniture had not rejected her.

Mio noticed, of course.

“Don’t make that face,” she said.

“What face?”

“The face where you look like you’ve just survived something.”

Touma looked away. “Maybe I have.”

The room went quiet enough for the air conditioner to become noticeable.

Touma hated that quiet.

He hated that it contained the shape of all the things he had not asked yet.

The joke version of this situation would have been easy. He could have insulted her. She could have insulted him back. Nico could have pretended this was all a technical issue. They could have stacked embarrassment until it became almost funny.

And that was part of what kept happening, because embarrassment was the only thing any of them knew how to use when a situation became too intimate to survive directly.

But there was another thing sitting under the jokes now.

Fear.

Mio’s fear wasn’t loud. It never had been. But Touma could see it now in the way she kept checking the room when she thought nobody was looking.

She was afraid of being perceived wrong.

He knew that fear. He knew it in the way he knew cold metal against skin and the feeling of saying the wrong thing in front of someone who could turn it into a life sentence.

He just hadn’t expected it to look so much like embarrassment when it stood up and wore a face.

Touma rubbed the back of his neck and tried to think of something normal to say.

“Can you eat?” he asked.

Nico looked up instantly. Mio blinked.

Touma regretted it at once.

“I mean—” he started.

“I know what you mean,” Mio said, and there was a dangerous softness in her voice now.

Touma shut up.

Mio looked down at her own hand again, more slowly this time. “I don’t know yet.”

He stared at her.

That answer should not have mattered so much.

It did anyway.

Because it meant she had a body-shaped set of unknowns. It meant the world had become capable of confusing her in ways he could not immediately solve. It meant there were going to be moments where she needed something and neither of them would know whether the answer was yes.

Touma hated how quickly his brain tried to inventory solutions.

He also hated how badly he wanted to take her hand.

The impulse came with so much force that he nearly stood up just from the effort of not acting on it.

Mio saw the movement in his posture and stiffened.

That made him stop.

“Oh,” she said quietly.

“What?”

“You were about to ask.”

Touma’s ears went hot. “Ask what?”

“If you can touch me.”

He went silent.

Nico, who had the decency to look away, said, “I am going to check the hallway.”

Touma pointed at him without looking. “No, you are not.”

“I am.”

“You’re leaving because you’re uncomfortable.”

“Yes.”

“That’s cowardly.”

“That’s mature.”

Nico opened the door anyway and slipped out before Touma could find a comeback sharp enough to justify a homicide.

The door shut.

The room became smaller immediately.

Touma wished very strongly that Nico had not done that.

Mio sat very still in the chair. She had the look of someone trying not to admit she had been waiting for the room to empty.

That was bad.

Touma set both hands on his knees. “I was going to ask if it hurt.”

Mio blinked at him.

He pressed on before he could lose the nerve. “I mean when I touch you. Or when things touch you. Or when the room does that weird half-glitch thing.”

Her expression changed by a fraction.

There it was again. The tiniest crack in the armor.

“I don’t know,” she said.

Touma let out a breath.

“That’s not good.”

“No,” Mio said. “It isn’t.”

He looked at her, really looked.

She was trying too hard to hold herself in a shape that still made sense to her. That was the thing no one ever said about people who were scared: they often looked most composed when they were actively trying not to fall apart.

Mio’s fingers twitched once against the chair arm.

Touma noticed immediately.

She noticed him noticing.

“You are making that face again,” she said.

“What face?”

“The one where you think feeling bad is the same as being useful.”

He stared at her.

“That’s an unfairly specific insult.”

“It is an accurate one.”

Touma almost smiled and then didn’t, because the whole situation was still absurd enough to be funny in the worst possible way.

He dragged a hand down his face.

“Okay,” he said. “We need ground rules.”

Mio looked suspicious immediately. “That sounds like the beginning of a nightmare.”

“It’s the beginning of me not tripping over you in the hallway and pretending that’s fine.”

“I did not ask to be visible.”

“I know.”

The bluntness of the answer seemed to catch her off guard.

Touma took the chance before his courage ran out.

“I know you didn’t ask,” he said more quietly. “I know you’re not doing this on purpose.”

Mio looked down.

That tiny movement did more to him than any argument would have.

He looked away first, because if he kept staring he was going to make the kind of expression she would mock for the next week.

“Rule one,” he said. “No surprise touching.”

Mio snorted once. “That is not a rule. That is basic decency.”

“Fine. Rule one is you don’t make fun of me when I forget the obvious.”

“Then I will need a stronger rule.”

“Rule two,” Touma said, “you tell me if something hurts.”

Mio went very still.

Touma hated how much that mattered.

“I don’t want to be a problem,” she said, and it came out too fast to be casual.

Touma looked at her.

There it was. The fear under the comedy. The one she kept trying to bury under sarcasm and precision and that infuriating habit of acting like she only existed if she was currently performing a function.

He swallowed.

“You are already a problem,” he said.

Mio’s mouth twitched like she wanted to hit him with a chair.

He held up one hand. “I mean that in the normal way. The way people are problems when they exist near each other and start affecting each other’s decisions. You know. Humanly.”

“That is a terrible explanation.”

“It’s true, though.”

She looked at him for a long second.

Then, very softly, “Touma.”

He stiffened.

She rarely used his name without a bite in it. Hearing it like that made him feel exposed in a way he didn’t have words for.

“What?” he asked.

Mio’s gaze shifted to the floor, then back up. “If I say something is wrong, you will listen?”

Touma answered too quickly. “Yes.”

She studied him.

He realized, with a sick little lurch, that she did not entirely believe him.

That was fair.

He had spent too much of his life bluffing and too much of his relationship with her insisting she was the annoying part of a survival system instead of the reason he had kept surviving inside it.

Touma forced himself to keep eye contact.

“Yes,” he repeated. “I’ll listen.”

Mio’s expression shifted.

It was not trust yet.

It was the beginning of wanting to believe trust might be less dangerous than it looked.

That was almost harder to see.

The door opened again before Touma could think of anything smart to say.

This time it wasn’t Nico.

It was the support tech from the adjoining hall, a young man with a clipboard and the immediate face of someone who had just walked into the wrong kind of miracle.

He stopped dead when he saw Mio.

Touma’s entire body went tight.

The tech’s eyes widened. “Uh—”

Mio looked at him.

For a second, she looked exactly like the thing Touma had feared and wanted and protected and accidentally brought into being.

Visible.

The tech’s mouth worked once. Then he pointed, with the helpless horror of a person whose instincts had all failed at once.

“Who is that?”

Mio went perfectly still.

Touma heard the blood rush in his ears.

The tech looked from Mio to Touma and back again, already building the wrong story in real time.

Mio’s shoulders tightened.

Touma knew that posture now.

It was the posture of someone trying not to be classified.

Touma stood up.

Too fast.

The tech flinched.

Touma did not care.

“That,” he said, and even he could hear how bad that sounded, “is not your business.”

The tech stared.

Mio shut her eyes for half a second.

Touma saw the embarrassment hit her before the fear did, and he hated the universe for making those two things arrive together.

The tech looked at the empty hallway behind him, then back at Touma, and his expression changed into something much worse than confusion.

Recognition.

Not of Mio.

Of gossip.

Touma knew the moment it happened because the young man’s face did that tiny, doomed shift people get when they realize they have just stepped into a story and may never escape being part of it.

“Oh,” the tech said, with the exact tone of someone who had already decided there would be a version of this anecdote later. “I—right. Sorry. I just needed to—”

“Leave,” Touma said.

The tech left.

The door shut.

The room was quiet for one long, horrible beat.

Then Mio, still staring at the doorway, said, in a voice so flat it almost counted as a scream, “That was public.”

Touma pressed both hands to his face.

“Yes.”

“That was visible.”

“Yes.”

“That was humiliating.”

“Yes.”

He heard her inhale once.

Then, because she was Mio and she could not let a catastrophic emotional event go by without making him suffer for it, she added, “I hope you enjoyed your dramatic reveal.”

Touma let out a noise that was halfway between a laugh and a groan.

“I hate you.”

“Incorrect.”

He lowered his hands and looked at her. She was trying so hard to look annoyed that the fear underneath it became even clearer.

Touma felt his own embarrassment sharpen into something protective.

Too many eyes.

Too many people.

Too much room for this to become a thing.

A rumor.

A weird story.

A problem.

He hated that it was already too late for the first two.

Mio folded her arms. “What do we do if people start asking?”

Touma stared at her.

He had been expecting sarcasm. Maybe even panic.

Not that.

Not the practical question.

The one that meant she was already thinking about the shape of the future embarrassment, because if she did not think about it first then somebody else would decide what it meant.

Touma’s throat tightened.

“We lie,” he said automatically.

Mio looked at him with a disgust so sincere it almost qualified as affection. “That was your plan?”

“I am open to revisions.”

“Badly.”

“You have no idea how badly.”

She looked at the floor for a second and then back up, and there it was again: the tiny, stubborn expression she wore when fear tried to occupy the same space as humor and she refused to let it win cleanly.

Touma had seen that expression in his head so many times that seeing it on her face now felt like getting punched by a memory he hadn’t earned yet.

Mio said, “If I am going to be seen, I refuse to be seen as a mistake.”

Touma went still.

The room, the safehouse, the stupid hallway outside the stupid door—everything narrowed around that sentence.

Because that was it, wasn’t it.

Not the visible problem.

The real problem.

If she was visible, then other people would start treating her like a question that needed answering.

Was she real?
Was she an implant side effect?
Was she his symptom?
Was she useful?
Was she safe?
Was she a threat?

Touma looked at her and felt the embarrassment burn off into something fiercer.

“No,” he said.

Mio blinked.

He stepped toward her, then stopped exactly where he had promised himself he would stop—close enough to be there, not so close as to pretend the boundary didn’t matter.

“You’re not a mistake,” he said.

Her expression changed.

Just for a second.

The sarcasm almost returned, but not quite.

“That was dangerously sincere,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“You should apologize.”

“No.”

“Rude.”

“Publicly visible,” Touma said, with great bitterness. “You’ve already ruined my image. We’re past manners.”

Mio stared at him.

Then, despite everything, she laughed.

It was small.

It was shaky.

It sounded like fear trying to pass itself off as mockery because it had nowhere else to go.

Touma felt the laugh land in his chest like something warm and dangerous.

For one very brief second, the room became bearable again.

Then the hallway outside the room made a soft scraping sound.

Not footsteps.

Not a door.

Something gentler than that.

Like a fingertip dragging along the other side of a wall.

Touma froze.

Mio’s laughter died instantly.

Her head snapped toward the sound.

The wall beside the bedroom door seemed, impossibly, to take on a faint pressure in the plaster.

The safehouse lights flickered once.

Touma felt the old familiar cold coin of the limiter behind his skull pulse in a way that had never felt so much like warning.

Mio’s voice dropped to a whisper.

“Touma,” she said, suddenly and unmistakably afraid, “don’t let the system decide what I am.”

And then the wall answered.

Not with words.

With a second, deeper scrape from inside the plaster, as if something on the other side had heard her and was leaning closer.
